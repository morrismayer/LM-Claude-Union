## LinkedIn Post 1 — Material costs and lead times

A note for the designers and hospitality purchasers I work with: material
costs and import timelines on fabric, trim, and certain hardware components
have been genuinely unpredictable over the last couple of years. Most of
what goes into custom drapery and upholstery still moves through
international supply chains, and "it'll ship on time" is no longer a safe
default assumption.

This isn't a reason to alarm a client. It's a reason to build realistic
buffer into project timelines and to work with a workroom that tells you
honestly what's in stock versus what's weeks out — before you commit a
schedule to a client, not after.

The workroom relationships that hold up best in a market like this are the
ones built on that kind of honesty. Worth asking your vendors directly: what
do you actually have on hand right now?

## LinkedIn Post 2 — Hospitality is not residential with nicer fabric

Hospitality and residential upholstery and drapery look similar on a spec
sheet. They don't fabricate the same way, and treating them identically is
where projects run into trouble.

A hotel lobby or restaurant banquette sees daily public use at a volume no
residential living room approaches. That changes foam density and frame
construction on upholstery, and it raises the bar on drapery hardware —
ripplefold track and heavier-duty brackets earn their cost when something is
opened and closed dozens of times a day instead of a handful of times a week.
Flammability and durability standards often apply differently, too.

None of this makes hospitality work harder to spec correctly. It means the
defaults are different, and a workroom that handles both regularly should be
applying the right ones without a designer having to spell out every
duty-cycle assumption.

## LinkedIn Post 3 — Why we fabricate drapery and upholstery under one roof

Most projects that include both window treatments and upholstered furniture
end up coordinated across two vendors, two timelines, and two quality
standards — which means the designer is the one absorbing the coordination
cost when they don't line up.

We've built Plaza Park Interiors around doing both in the same ~6,000 sq ft
workroom in Mamaroneck, NY, with field measuring and installation kept
in-house rather than handed to a third party. The team that measures the
window is the team that fabricates the panel and the team that installs it —
so when an adjustment is needed, there's one point of contact, not three
companies pointing at each other.

If your next project has both drapery and upholstery on the spec sheet, I'm
happy to talk through how we'd sequence it. If you have questions or need
clarity, I'm here to help — just reach out.
