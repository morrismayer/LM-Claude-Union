# The Workroom Side of the Spec Sheet: Fabric, Trim, Hardware, and What Actually Makes a Drapery or Upholstery Job Succeed

A designer hands me a spec sheet and I can tell, almost immediately, how the
job is going to go. Not because the fabric is wrong or the silhouette is
ambitious — those are the easy parts to fix. I can tell because of what's
*missing*: the yardage hasn't been checked against the pattern repeat, the
trim was specified without anyone confirming it could actually be applied the
way it was drawn, the hardware was picked for how it looks in a rendering, not
for how it's going to perform over five years of opening and closing.

None of that is a knock on the designer. Design school teaches you to see a
room. It doesn't teach you what happens to a bolt of fabric between the
moment it leaves the showroom and the moment it's hanging in 8-way hand-tied
pleats over a client's living room window. That gap is exactly where a
workroom earns its keep — or doesn't.

I've been doing this since the late 1980s. Plaza Park Interiors, the
business, is younger than I am in the trade — we opened the doors in 2013 —
but the hands doing the work have nearly four decades of cutting tables behind
them. What I want to do here is walk through the actual materials and
mechanics — fabric, trim, upholstery supplies, drapery hardware — the way we
think about them on the workroom floor, so the next spec sheet you send over
has fewer surprises waiting on the other end of it.

## Fabric: the part everyone gets right and the part almost no one checks

Fabric selection gets a lot of attention, and rightly so — it's the part of
the room everyone sees. What gets less attention is what happens to that
fabric *before* it becomes a finished panel or a cushion cover, and that's
where projects either stay on budget or quietly blow past it.

Two things matter more than people expect: railroaded goods and pattern
repeat. A railroaded fabric runs its pattern sideways on the bolt, which means
for wide drapery panels we can often avoid seaming across the width — cleaner
look, fewer visible seams, sometimes less fabric wasted. Not every fabric is
railroaded, and not every railroaded fabric should be used that way for every
application, but it's a question worth asking your workroom before you fall
in love with a specific yardage number, because the answer changes the math.

Pattern repeat is the other one. A large-repeat fabric — think a big floral
or a wide-set stripe — needs extra yardage so the pattern lines up panel to
panel and matches at the seams. Skip that step and you get a drapery pair
where the motif is offset by three inches at eye level. It's the kind of
mistake that's invisible on a swatch and impossible to miss once the panels
are hung.

We work with fabric houses like Romo and Calico regularly, and we handle COM
— customer's own material — just as often, where a designer sources fabric
independently and ships it to us to fabricate. COM is great for control and
sourcing flexibility, but it puts the yardage-and-repeat math entirely on
whoever ordered the goods. If you're working COM, loop your workroom in
*before* you place the fabric order, not after — we'd rather tell you "order
two more yards" before the bolt ships than tell you "we're a yard short" after
it's not coming back.

## Trim: the detail that makes or breaks the silhouette

Trim is where a lot of good drapery and upholstery specs lose their nerve.
It's specified on a sample board, looks beautiful next to the fabric swatch,
and then gets quietly dropped or substituted because nobody confirmed how it
would actually be applied.

We do a fair amount of work with trim houses like Samuel and Sons, and the
thing I'd tell any designer is: trim isn't one decision, it's three. There's
the trim itself — tape, cord, tassel fringe, banding. There's the application
method — glued, which is faster and fine for some upholstery applications but
won't hold up the same way on heavily used pieces, versus sewn into the seam,
which costs more in labor but moves with the fabric instead of separating
from it. And there's the placement — leading edge, hem, a banded inset a few
inches in from the edge of a panel, which reads completely differently than
trim right at the edge.

Get those three decisions made early, with the workroom in the loop, and trim
becomes the thing that elevates a piece. Leave them until fabrication and
you'll either get a substitution you didn't choose or a piece that doesn't
look like the rendering. Trim yardage also isn't free-form — banding and tape
get measured and ordered against the actual perimeter being trimmed, with
overage for application, the same disciplined way we calculate fabric.

## Drapery hardware: the part nobody thinks about until it fails

If fabric is what a client falls in love with, hardware is what they call us
about three years later. Hardware doesn't get the design attention it
deserves because, when it's right, nobody notices it. When it's wrong, it's
the only thing anyone notices.

Two hardware decisions drive almost everything else: the track or rod system,
and the fullness ratio. Ripplefold track systems give you that clean, evenly
spaced wave with no visible pleats or rings — great for a modern look and for
drapery that gets opened and closed often, since the carriers glide instead
of dragging rings across a pole. Traditional rod-and-ring setups with pinch
pleats give you more texture and a more classic silhouette, but the hardware
quality matters enormously, because rings and brackets are exactly what takes
the daily wear.

Fullness ratio — how much fabric width you're using relative to the finished
track or rod length — is the single most under-specified number on a lot of
drapery jobs. Too little fullness and a drapery looks flat and cheap no
matter how good the fabric is. Get it right — generally more fabric than
people instinctively budget for — and even a modest fabric looks intentional
and full. This is a number we calculate, not eyeball, because it determines
both how the finished piece looks and exactly how much fabric to order.

And hardware quality is where cheap installations fail first. It's rarely the
fabric that goes first on a drapery treatment — it's a bracket that was never
rated for the weight of the panel, or rings that wear through after a few
thousand open-close cycles. Specify hardware like it has to last, because it
does.

## Upholstery supplies: what's actually inside the piece

Upholstery is even less visible than drapery hardware, because almost
everything that matters is inside the piece. The fabric is doing all the
visual work; the construction underneath is doing all the durability work.

8-way hand-tied construction is the benchmark for a reason: every spring is
individually tied in eight directions to the frame, which spreads weight and
movement evenly and keeps the seating surface performing the same way after
years of use, instead of sagging in the spots people sit most. It costs more
in labor than alternatives, and it's worth it on a piece meant to be lived
with rather than replaced.

Foam density and fill matter just as much and get specified even less. The
right density for a tight-back sofa cushion is different from what you'd want
in a deep-seat lounge chair, and getting it wrong shows up within a season as
either a cushion that's gone soft or one that never felt right to begin with.

And then there's the math that almost nobody outside a workroom thinks about:
cushion yardage. We calculate it methodically — figure the cushion's surface
area, convert that to square yards, round up to the nearest quarter yard so
there's no coming up short mid-cut, then adjust for the fabric's pattern
repeat and for seam allowance if the boxing is seamed rather than one
continuous wrap. It's a small, unglamorous calculation, and it's the
difference between a clean quote and a change order halfway through
fabrication. Outdoor cushions, upholstered headboards, custom bedding
ensembles — every one of them runs through the same discipline.

## Material costs and lead times: the part nobody puts on the moodboard

It would be dishonest to write about fabric and trim and hardware right now
without mentioning the obvious: material costs and import timelines have been
moving, and a lot of the goods that go into custom drapery and upholstery —
fabric, trim, certain hardware components — come through international
supply chains that have been anything but predictable the last couple of
years. That's not a reason to panic a client. It's a reason to build a little
more buffer into the schedule than habit tells you to, and to ask your
workroom early what's actually in stock versus what has to be ordered in.

The designers who weather this best aren't the ones who ignore it — they're
the ones who quote lead time honestly up front and use a workroom that tells
them the truth about what's sitting on the shelf versus what's six weeks out.
A workroom relationship that's been around the block a few times is worth
more in a market like this than it was when everything shipped on time by
default. That's true whether the goods are coming from a fabric house, a trim
house, or a hardware supplier — ask, don't assume.

## Hospitality work plays by different rules

Residential and hospitality projects look similar on a spec sheet and
fabricate completely differently. A hospitality job — a hotel lobby, a
restaurant banquette, a boutique inn's guest rooms — has to survive daily
public use at a scale no residential living room ever will, which changes
almost every material decision underneath the fabric.

Upholstery for hospitality typically needs to meet flammability and
durability standards a residential piece doesn't, which affects foam choice,
frame construction, and sometimes the fabric itself. Drapery hardware in a
hospitality setting gets opened and closed by staff or guests dozens of times
a day rather than a handful of times a week, so the case for ripplefold track
and heavier-duty brackets gets stronger, not weaker. And turnaround matters
differently — a hotel doesn't have the luxury of a guest room sitting
half-finished the way a homeowner might tolerate a slow living room reno.

None of that means hospitality work is harder to get right. It means it's a
different set of defaults, and a workroom that does both residential and
hospitality regularly should be applying the right defaults without the
designer having to spell out every flammability code and duty cycle on the
spec sheet.

## Why one workroom for both drapery and upholstery actually matters

Here's the part that's more business reality than craft detail, but it
affects every project: drapery and upholstery on the same job, fabricated by
two different vendors, means two different timelines, two different quality
standards, and two different people to call when something doesn't match.
We do both under one roof — same workroom, same standards, same point of
contact — specifically so a designer juggling a full room isn't also
juggling vendor coordination on top of it.

It also means field measuring and installation stay in-house rather than
getting handed off to a third party. The team that measured the window is
the team that fabricated the panel and the team that's hanging it — so when
something needs an adjustment, there's no finger-pointing between three
companies about whose measurement was off.

## What this actually means for your next spec

None of this is meant to make fabric and trim and hardware sound
complicated for the sake of it — it's meant to save you a change order. Ask
your workroom about railroading and repeat before you finalize yardage. Lock
in trim application method before fabrication starts, not during. Specify
hardware and fullness ratio like the piece needs to survive daily use,
because it does. And loop the workroom in early on COM, because the yardage
math only works if everyone's doing it before the fabric ships, not after.
