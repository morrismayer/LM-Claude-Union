## Post 2 — Fabric and COM

Working with customer's own material (COM) gives a designer total control
over sourcing. It also puts the yardage math entirely on whoever placed that
fabric order — pattern repeat, railroading, all of it.

Our rule: loop your workroom in before the fabric ships, not after. We'd
rather tell you to order two more yards before the bolt's cut than tell you
we're a yard short once it's not coming back.

## Post 3 — Trim is three decisions, not one

Trim looks great on a sample board and then quietly disappears by the time a
piece is fabricated — usually because nobody decided how it would actually be
applied.

Three decisions, every time: the trim itself (tape, cord, tassel fringe,
banding), the application (glued vs. sewn into the seam), and the placement
(leading edge, hem, or a banded inset). Make all three early and trim
elevates the piece instead of becoming a last-minute substitution.

drapery edge]

## Post 4 — The hardware nobody thinks about until it fails

Fabric is what a client falls in love with. Hardware is what they call us
about three years later.

Ripplefold track or traditional rod-and-ring with pinch pleats — either can
be right. What's never optional is fullness ratio (how much fabric width
relative to the finished rod length) and hardware quality, because rings and
brackets are exactly what takes the daily wear. We calculate fullness; we
don't eyeball it.

## Post 5 — What's inside the upholstery matters more than what's on top

Everything visible on an upholstered piece is doing the design work. Almost
everything that matters for how long it lasts is hidden inside it.

8-way hand-tied construction ties every spring individually in eight
directions, spreading weight evenly so the seat performs the same years in —
not just on delivery day. We calculate cushion yardage the same disciplined
way: area to square yards, rounded up, adjusted for pattern repeat and seam
allowance. Small math, big difference at fabrication.
